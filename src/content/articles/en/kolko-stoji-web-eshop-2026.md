As a rough guide: a simple landing page starts from around €600, a company website with an admin panel from €1,000, and an online store with payments and inventory from €2,000. The exact number for your project only comes out of a proper brief — because price isn't driven by whether you want "a website," but by what you actually need it to do.

I get this question in my inbox almost every week, and "it depends" satisfies nobody. So let's break down what it actually depends on, with concrete numbers you can plan around.

## What determines the price (it's not page count)

The most common mistake when estimating price is counting pages. In practice, three completely different factors drive the cost:

**Scope and functionality.** A static presentational page with no admin panel is cheaper than a site where you edit copy and publish posts yourself through a CMS. An online store with its own logic (product variants, discount coupons, shipping calculations) is a different league from a catalog with a few dozen products.

**Custom design vs. a template.** A ready-made template deploys fast and cheap, but looks like hundreds of other sites and is hard to adapt exactly to your brand. Custom design costs more hours (wireframing, design rounds, revisions), but the result fits precisely what you need — and typically converts better, because it's built around your customer instead of a generic pattern.

**Integrations.** This is the most common reason two "similar-looking" projects end up priced very differently. A payment gateway, inventory or accounting system integration (e.g. Pohoda, MoneyS3 for the Slovak market), a CRM, email marketing — each integration is more work to build, test, and maintain. For an online store, more products and more complex logistics mean more work on imports, stock-status syncing, and edge cases.

## Why a store costs more than a company website

The jump from €1,000 (company website) to €2,000 (online store) isn't arbitrary — it's a jump in complexity, not just "a few extra features." A company website essentially has one type of content: pages and posts that someone writes and someone reads. A store adds an entire transactional flow that has to work flawlessly, because customers' money moves through it.

Concretely, that means: the payment gateway has to be integrated and tested (including edge cases like a failed payment, a return, or a partial cancellation), inventory or ERP data has to stay current so you're not selling something you don't have, and the checkout flow has to be tuned so it doesn't scare people off — I cover the micro-adjustments that actually move conversion in a [separate article](/en/blog/checkout-konvertuje-9-uprav/). On top of that comes product count: a store with 50 products is far simpler to launch than one with 5,000, where you're also dealing with data import, categorization, filtering, and performance at catalog scale.

## Why a "cheap website for €200" often ends up costing more

Offers for a few hundred euros exist, and sometimes they make sense — say, if you genuinely just need a simple business card site with no bigger ambitions. The problem is when someone assumes that for €200 they'll get the same thing as for €1,000, just cheaper.

In practice that usually means one (or more) of the following: a template you can't customize yourself without another round of paid work; no performance optimization, so the site is slow and ranks worse in search; no support after handover, so every small change becomes a new project with a new vendor; or security and SEO basics that got skipped to hit the price, which you end up paying for later — during a migration, after a hack, or simply because nobody can find the site.

A cheap website isn't automatically a bad decision. The bad decision is not knowing exactly what you're getting for that price, and what's missing from it.

A good test: ask the vendor directly what exactly isn't included in that price. Anyone putting together a proper quote can answer that on the spot — it's written into the price list or the contract. If the answer is vague, or only surfaces after you've signed, that's a bigger warning sign than the low price itself.

## What's typically included in the price

A proper quote should make it clear exactly what you're getting — not just "a website," but a concrete list. Typically that includes:

- **Design** — wireframing and at least one revision round based on feedback.
- **Development** — the actual build, tested across devices and browsers.
- **Deployment** — hosting setup, domain, SSL, basic SEO (sitemap, meta descriptions).
- **Post-launch support** — a time-boxed period for small fixes and adjustments without a separate invoice.

What's usually not included: bespoke copywriting for your content (typically you supply it, or it's billed separately), product photography, and ongoing marketing (SEO campaigns, paid ads) once the support period ends.

## How to get an exact quote for your project

An exact number only comes out of a proper brief — a short conversation about what the site needs to do, for whom, and with what integrations. The process I usually run is a 30-minute [discovery call](/en/blog/discovery-call-30-minut/) that covers 8 key questions — no lengthy technical pitch, no premature pricing promises. After that call you know whether you're moving forward, and I know which package (or combination) to propose.

If you're not sure where to start — whether you need an entirely new site, or just need to fix what you already have — the cheapest first step is an audit of your existing site from €300. You get a concrete list of what to fix, and only then decide on a bigger investment.

Before the discovery call, it helps to have three things roughly sketched out: a budget range (even a rough one — it immediately rules out unrealistic combinations), a list of "must-have" features kept separate from "nice-to-have," and a few examples of sites or stores you like (competitors included). The more concrete the brief, the faster and more precise the quote on the other end.
