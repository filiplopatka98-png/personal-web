"Webnode or WordPress?" comes up most often with small businesses that want their first website, or wonder whether the one they have is still enough. Both work. They differ in **how much work they save you at the start and how much freedom they leave you later**. I am writing this as someone who builds and maintains WordPress sites, so I take the practical view, not the marketing one.

## In short: what is the difference

**Webnode** is a hosted website builder. Everything from the editor to hosting and templates runs at the provider. You assemble the site from ready-made blocks and pay a subscription.

**WordPress** is an open-source system that you install on your own hosting. Design, features and data are under your control, but someone has to take care of hosting, updates and backups.

Put simply: Webnode is like renting a flat (you move in fast, but you cannot rearrange the walls or take them with you), WordPress is like owning a house (more work, more freedom).

## Where Webnode makes sense

- **A quick start without technical skills.** A business card or small company site you can launch yourself in a few hours.
- **You do not want to manage anything.** Hosting, updates and security are handled by the provider.
- **The budget is minimal** and the site only needs to present the basics: who you are, what you do, contact details.
- **Smaller multilingual sites.** According to public overviews, Webnode offers multilingual support in higher plans (two languages in Standard, more in Profi), so a simple bilingual site can be done without plugins.

If this is exactly your situation, there is no need to complicate things. For many small businesses it is a reasonable choice.

## Where you hit limits

These are not flaws of Webnode, they are consequences of it being a closed tool:

- **Ads and features depend on the plan.** The free and cheaper plans show a Webnode banner, and some features (such as an online store, Google Analytics integration or backups) are only in higher plans. Check the exact split in the current price list, it changes.
- **Limited control over design and code.** You change the look within what the editor allows. Custom logic, unusual page behaviour or special integrations are often a problem.
- **SEO only at a basic level.** You can set titles, descriptions, addresses and a sitemap. For more advanced SEO (redirects, structure, performance) you may lack control. According to some reviews, creating 301 redirects is limited, so check this with the provider before you decide.
- **Growth.** When the site needs more than a presentation (bookings, B2B prices, custom filters, accounting integration), you hit a ceiling.
- **Dependence on the provider.** The site runs at Webnode. If prices, terms or plans change, your room to manoeuvre is limited.

## What WordPress gives you

- **Control.** Hosting, design, code and data are yours. The site is not locked into one company.
- **Extensibility.** From an online store ([WooCommerce](/en/blog/co-je-woocommerce-pre-koho/)) through bookings to integrations with other systems.
- **SEO and performance.** You can work on technical SEO, URL structure, redirects and speed. See [SEO for WordPress and WooCommerce](/en/blog/seo-wordpress-woocommerce-zaklad/) and [Site speed vs. SEO](/en/blog/seo-rychlost-webu-poradie/).
- **Ownership of content.** The data is with you and you can move it elsewhere.

## What WordPress does not give you for free

To be fair, WordPress has its price too:

- **Build.** A custom-built site costs more than a template in a builder. Indicative prices: [Website and e-shop pricing in Slovakia (2026)](/en/blog/kolko-stoji-web-eshop-2026/).
- **Hosting.** Slow or cheap hosting shows up in speed.
- **Maintenance.** Updates, backups, security. Someone has to do it, either you or a maintainer.
- **Responsibility.** If something breaks, you (or your maintainer) deal with it.

## Side by side

| | Webnode | WordPress |
|---|---|---|
| Launch speed | hours to days | days to weeks (custom) |
| Technical skills | minimal | needed (or a developer) |
| Starting cost | low | higher (build) |
| Running costs | subscription | hosting + maintenance |
| Control over design and code | limited | full |
| SEO and performance | basic | full control |
| Online store and special features | limited by plan | wide options |
| Moving elsewhere | harder | easier |

## If you already have a Webnode site and want to move to WordPress

It can be done, but plan it as a small project, not an "export click":

1. **Inventory.** List all pages with their addresses (URLs), titles and descriptions.
2. **Content and images.** Text and images are moved manually or semi-automatically, and the design is rebuilt.
3. **Addresses and redirects.** If page addresses change, set up 301 redirects from the old ones to the new ones. Otherwise you lose Google rankings and incoming links.
4. **Test before switching.** Check forms, the map, e-mail and speed on the new site.
5. **Switch the domain** only when the new site is ready.

The most common mistake when moving from one system to another is not the technology but forgotten redirects. In a migration of an e-shop from OpenCart to WooCommerce, the outcome was decided by remapping the old addresses, not by the system itself.

## How to decide

Ask yourself:

- **Is a business card that I will not change enough?** Webnode is a reasonable start.
- **Do I want the site to bring enquiries over time** (SEO, content, speed)? Choose WordPress.
- **Will I need an online store or special features?** WordPress.
- **Do I not want to deal with the technical side and have nobody to hand it to?** Webnode, or WordPress with a maintainer.
- **Do I plan to grow and not want to move in a few years?** WordPress.

There is no universally right answer. There is the one that fits your scope, budget and willingness to look after a site (or pay someone who will).
